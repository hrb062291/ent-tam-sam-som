How big is your opportunity, really?

Every founder eventually gets asked, "How big is this market?" TAM, SAM, and SOM are three nested numbers that answer that question honestly — from the whole universe of demand down to the slice you can realistically win.

Part 1

Three circles, three questions

Each circle sits inside the one before it. You move inward by applying honest filters: Can we reach them? Does our product fit them? Can we actually win them?

TAM

Total Addressable Market

"If every possible customer bought, how much would they spend per year?"

The total annual demand for the problem you solve — ignoring competition, geography, and your limits.

  • Shows the ceiling of the opportunity
  • Usually a big, abstract number
SAM

Serviceable Available Market

"Which part of that can our business model actually serve?"

The slice of TAM that fits your product, location, channel, and price point.

  • Filters: geography, customer type, language, regulation, price
  • Your "home field"
SOM

Serviceable Obtainable Market

"How much of the SAM can we realistically capture in 3–5 years?"

Your realistic share given competitors, budget, team, and time.

  • Should be backed by a sales/marketing plan
  • This is what goes in your revenue forecast
Analogy — opening a pizza shop: TAM is all the money Americans spend on pizza each year. SAM is what people within delivery range of your shop spend on pizza. SOM is the share of those local pizza dollars you can realistically pull away from Domino's, the place down the street, and frozen pizza — given one oven, two drivers, and your marketing budget.

Part 3

Top-down vs. bottom-up

There are two ways to get to TAM. Investors trust bottom-up more, because it's built from things you can actually verify — real customers and real prices. The best pitches do both and show they land in the same neighborhood.

Try it: In the calculator, switch between Bottom-up and Top-down for the same idea. Does the industry-report number line up with customers × spend? If not, which assumption is off?

Lean Canvas tie-in: Your TAM/SAM/SOM should come straight from your Customer Segments box (who) and your Revenue Streams box (how much they pay). If your SAM filters don't match the segment you described, one of them needs to change.

Part 4

Reality check: can you actually win your SOM?

A SOM is only believable if you can show how you'd get those customers. Compare the customers your SOM requires against how many your sales and marketing engine can realistically win.

Think about where "per month" comes from: How many sales calls, flyers, ads, or events? What % convert? E.g., 400 people see your cart daily × 2% become regulars ≈ 8 new regulars a day at launch, but that slows once you've reached most of campus.

Part 6

Common mistakes (that investors spot instantly)

Click each one to see why it hurts your credibility.

The "1% of China" trap

"There are 1.4 billion people in China — if we get just 1%, we're rich!" This skips SAM and SOM entirely and offers no reason anyone would buy. A small, well-defended SOM beats a huge, hand-wavy one every time.

Counting people you can't reach

If you only sell in Sacramento, U.S.-wide customers belong in TAM, not SAM. Your SAM must reflect your real geography, channels, language, and price point today.

Defining the market by your product instead of the problem

"The market for AI-powered dog-walking apps" is tiny or doesn't exist yet. Define TAM by the need: what people spend on getting their dogs walked, however they solve it today.

A SOM with no plan behind it

Picking 10% because it "sounds reasonable" isn't analysis. Your SOM should connect to a go-to-market plan: channels, conversion rates, sales capacity, and budget (see the Reality Check above).

Mixing up units or time frames

Market size is usually revenue per year. Don't mix annual spend with lifetime value, or count customers in one circle and dollars in another.

Inflating TAM to look impressive

A giant TAM doesn't make a weak idea strong. Investors care that the market is big enough to matter and that you understand exactly which slice you're going after first.

Using stale or unrelated data

A 2015 report on "the food industry" doesn't size a 2026 vegan meal-kit business. Cite recent, specific sources — and triangulate with a bottom-up estimate.
